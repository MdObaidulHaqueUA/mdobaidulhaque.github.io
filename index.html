Focus Area

Research

Every few generations, a deadly pandemic reshapes society: the 1918 influenza caused an estimated fifty million deaths, and COVID-19 about fifteen million excess deaths in its first two years. Governments rely on models to respond; in March 2020, the Imperial College model's projections shaped the early lockdowns. Yet every such prediction depends on something very hard to model: how people behave.

Epidemics spread through people, along their real daily contacts, and classical models such as SEIR treat everyone as identical even though behavior is heterogeneous and adaptive. My research asks: once contact networks, individual behavioral choices, and the randomness of transmission are taken seriously, how much do epidemic outcomes change?

My first project embeds large language models (LLMs) as the behavioral engines of agents on a contact network. The LLM reads each agent's profile and local epidemic state and decides how strongly that person protects themselves, producing demographically structured infection burdens that homogeneous and rule-based baselines fail to reproduce.

My second project reformulates the framework as a continuous-time stochastic SEIRS model driven by independent Poisson clocks, and distills the costly LLM decisions into an interpretable Generalized Additive Model (GAM) that makes simulations with tens of thousands of agents feasible at no API cost.

My third project builds the mathematical foundations. For agents who switch protective behavior on and off, I derive martingale formulations on complete and incomplete networks that split each population quantity into a deterministic drift and a random fluctuation. I prove that these fluctuations shrink like 1/N, which yields the large-population limit on complete networks and a pair approximation on sparse ones, and shows how small populations and local contact structure make epidemics less predictable.

Experimental Results · 2026

Key Research Findings

Three connected projects build a stochastic framework for epidemic modeling with LLM-driven behavioral heterogeneity. Project I couples a networked SEIR model with GPT-4o-mini agents and shows how age, occupation, and income reshape the infection burden. Project II reformulates the model in continuous time with independent Poisson clocks and distills the LLM's decision rule into a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) surrogate for large-scale simulation at zero API cost. Project III develops martingale formulations, large-population limits, and pair approximations that connect agent-level behavior to population-level predictions.

The martingale decomposition separates stochastic fluctuation from deterministic drift along every sample path, with fluctuations shrinking like 1/N as the population grows. The merged Poisson superposition of all transition clocks yields a provably correct Gillespie algorithm.

What Comes Next

Future Contributions

The three research projects open into a broader agenda: validating behavioral realism against empirical evidence, scaling the simulation framework to policy-relevant population sizes, and strengthening its theoretical guarantees.

I

LLM-Driven Behavior: Validation & Realism

  • Validate LLM-generated compliance decisions against empirical survey data on protective behavior during COVID-19.
  • Benchmark behavioral fidelity across several LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Llama, Mistral) to test cross-model consistency.
  • Run the framework on empirical temporal contact networks such as hospitals, schools, and workplaces.
  • Separate real-world behavioral randomness from LLM sampling noise.
II

GAM Surrogate: Scale & Policy Design

  • Complete decision- and epidemic-level validation of the GAM across larger populations, parameters, and networks.
  • Benchmark the GAM against other surrogates (neural networks, random forests, gradient-boosted trees) on the interpretability–accuracy trade-off.
  • Scale to N = 50,000+ agents with full Monte Carlo sensitivity analyses at zero marginal API cost.
  • Use the surrogate for intervention design, searching over policy parameters to reduce epidemic burden.
III

Martingale Theory: Scaling & Closures

  • Extend the martingale framework from fixed protective behavior to the SEIRS model with GAM-driven decisions.
  • Establish the complete-graph large-N limit with an appropriate scaling of the meeting probability.
  • Derive degree-resolved dyad equations on multiplex networks and validate triple closures against stochastic simulations.
  • Extend pair approximations to heterogeneous and clustered networks.

The long-term goal is an open-source epidemic simulation toolkit that combines rigorous stochastic theory with LLM-driven behavioral realism at realistic population scales.
