Focus Area

Research

Infectious diseases don't spread through a statistical average — they spread through people, along the real contacts those people make every day. My research starts from that simple observation and asks: once you take heterogeneous contact networks, individual behavioral choices, and the randomness baked into every transmission event seriously, how much do the epidemic outcomes change?

My first research thread places the epidemic on a network where transmission requires physical contact, and lets each node respond to news, risk perception, and social pressure — not a fixed rate. To model that response realistically, I embed large language models (LLMs) as the behavioral engines driving each agent: the LLM reads the agent's demographic profile and the local epidemic state, then decides how that person actually behaves. This produces outbreak trajectories that mirror the heterogeneity seen in real data far better than homogeneous-mixing baselines.

Running an LLM for every agent at every time-step is expensive, so a third thread distills that behavioral intelligence into a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) surrogate. The GAM learns the LLM's decision surface from a modest set of simulation runs and then stands in for it at scale — preserving accuracy while reducing computational cost by orders of magnitude, making population-level studies tractable.

Alongside the simulation work, a fourth thread builds the mathematical foundations. I develop martingale decompositions for the SIR dynamics on both complete and incomplete networks, deriving exact stochastic representations and tracking the fluctuations that mean-field equations miss. Large-population scaling results then connect these finite-network processes to their fluid limits, giving rigorous error bounds and a principled route from the agent-level model to population-level predictions.

Key Research Findings

Using GPT-4o-mini agents in 100-run Monte Carlo experiments on a 500-agent networked SEIR model (N=500, 30 days, α=0.2, β=0.1, γ=0.05), LLM-driven behavioral decisions reveal striking heterogeneity across age, occupation, and income that uniform baseline models completely erase. Age and income together account for 84% of behavioral contribution to epidemic outcomes.

Non-LLM baselines give one attack rate for every group — they lower infections but erase the real disparities that decide who bears the epidemic burden. Only LLM-driven protection generates context-aware decisions that redistribute infection across social strata in alignment with real-world behavioral patterns.

Live LLM calls (~200 ms, ~$0.003 each) are infeasible at population scale. A GAM surrogate distilled from LLM decisions reproduces epidemic dynamics at zero API cost.

The GAM surrogate — fit by penalized least squares from 9 current-state inputs (age, income, health state, occupation, incidence, peer compliance, local risk, network degree, protection status) — reproduces full SEIRS trajectories with overlapping 95% confidence bands at N=100. Validation at larger scales and broader parameter settings is ongoing. "LLMs give agents a voice; a GAM surrogate lets that voice scale."
